Question 1

Where is revenue growth coming from: new customers, more frequent orders, bigger orders, or higher prices?

Why it matters

Revenue more than doubled. Whether that came from winning new customers, from existing customers buying more often, or from raising prices decides where the next dollar of sales effort should go and whether the growth can last.

Approach

Revenue is split exactly into active customers × orders per customer × average order value, comparing Jan–Apr 1997 with Jan–Apr 1998. Because the three factors multiply, each one's share of growth is its share of the log of total growth: ln(1.69) ÷ ln(2.21) ≈ 66% for orders per customer.

Average order value is further split into a price index and the remaining volume and product-mix effect. The price index values the 1998 basket at each product's quantity-weighted average price in both windows. It covers the 71 products sold in both windows; 3 products sold only in 1998 (2.1% of 1998 revenue) have no 1997 price and are left out of the index, so their effect falls into the volume and mix factor. Customers are also counted by the quarter of their first recorded order.

Main finding

Revenue rose from $191,322 to $422,290 (+121%). The main driver is existing customers ordering more often: orders per customer rose +69%, about 66% of the growth (active customers 26%, average order value 7%). Unit prices rose +19%, but orders contained fewer units or cheaper products, so the average order grew only +6%.

84% of Jan–Apr 1998 revenue came from customers who were already buying in Jan–Apr 1997. Few customers appear for the first time: only 5 customers have their first recorded order after June 1997, and 1 in 1998. The extra active customers are mostly earlier customers ordering again.

"First recorded order" means the first order in this dataset. Customers may have bought before July 1996, which is why the 1996 Q3 bar is so high.

Business implication

Northwind is growing by selling more to the same customers, while very few customers are appearing for the first time. Frequency gains cannot grow forever, so the manager should (1) invest in winning new customers; (2) protect the higher order frequency with standing orders and reorder reminders; and (3) watch whether the price increase is shrinking basket sizes.

Question 2

Which customer markets are growing or shrinking, and how much revenue is at risk?

Why it matters

Sales attention is limited. Knowing which markets carry the growth and which are slipping tells the manager where to put salespeople and where to intervene before revenue is lost.

Approach

Revenue by customer country, Jan–Apr 1998 vs the same months of 1997 (comparing the same months avoids mixing growth with seasonality). Revenue at risk is the shrinking countries' full-year 1997 revenue multiplied by their combined rate of decline, i.e. the annual loss if the decline continues. Because each shrinking country has only a few customers, the individual accounts are examined too.

Main finding

These four countries have only 12 customers between them, so the decline is really about single accounts. Simons bistro (Denmark) placed one unusually large order in January 1997 and is still ordering. The real warning sign is Mère Paillarde (Canada): $23,332 in 1997, but no order since 1997-10-30 (188 days).

Growth in the leading countries is also driven by a few accounts: USA: Save-a-lot Markets 38% of the country's growth, top two accounts 60%; Germany: QUICK-Stop 46% of the country's growth, top two accounts 80%; Brazil: Hanari Carnes 75% of the country's growth, top two accounts 90%.

Business implication

Manage key accounts, not countries. Contact Mère Paillarde now to find out why it stopped ordering, and set an alert for any top-30 customer that has not ordered in 90 days. The USA, Germany and Brazil deserve continued attention, but their growth depends on a handful of accounts (most of all Hanari Carnes in Brazil), so relationships with those accounts should be secured before the growth is treated as a market-wide trend.

Question 3

How dependent is Northwind on its largest customers, and is that dependence rising?

Why it matters

If a few customers bring in most of the revenue, losing one of them hurts badly. The answer affects whether the company needs dedicated key-account management and how urgently it must broaden its customer base.

Approach

Share of revenue from the top 10% of customers and from the top 3, in rolling 4-month windows. All windows have the same length because a shorter period looks more concentrated simply because fewer customers order in it. The growth of the largest customers is also compared with that of all others.

Main finding

Dependence is high and has stayed in a similar range since early 1997, with no clear upward trend. The top 10% of customers (8 companies) brought in 48% of revenue in Jan–Apr 1998, compared with 46% a year earlier, a small increase. The share rose from 36% in late 1996 and has moved between 41% and 51% since April 1997. The three largest customers (QUICK-Stop, Ernst Handel, Save-a-lot Markets) are about 25% of revenue.

The largest customers of early 1997 grew +55%, while all other customers grew +177%. The list of top accounts is changing. For example, Hanari Carnes (Brazil) has been a customer since 1996-07-08, but grew from $6,023 in all of 1997 to $23,821 in Jan–Apr 1998 alone.

Comparing whole calendar years instead (1996 H2, 1997, Jan–Apr 1998) would wrongly suggest a steady rise from 37% to 52%, because those periods have different lengths.

Business implication

No emergency, but real exposure: losing one of the top three would remove about 8% of revenue. Give the top three customers named account managers and regular business reviews. The Mère Paillarde case (the #9 customer of all time) shows that large accounts can go quiet without anyone noticing.

Question 4

Does discounting buy more volume, or does it just give away revenue?

Why it matters

Discounts reduced revenue by $88,666 (6.5% of list value) on 39% of order lines. If larger discounts do not come with larger orders, part of that revenue may be given up for little in return.

Approach

Each order line's quantity is compared with the average full-price quantity of the same product, so the result is not driven by which products get discounts. Error bars show 95% confidence intervals. An employee's discount rate is total discount dollars divided by the total list value (price × quantity) of the order lines they handled, so large lines count more than small ones. The discount dollars above a hypothetical 10% cap are also calculated.

Main finding

Discounted lines are 26%–41% larger than full-price lines of the same product, but larger discounts are not associated with clearly larger quantities: lines at 5% off are +33% larger, lines at 25% off +41%, and the confidence intervals overlap. Discounts above 10% appear on 21.9% of lines. The part of those discounts above a hypothetical 10% cap was $36,083. This is a historical amount at unchanged quantities, not revenue that a cap would be guaranteed to recover, because some customers might have bought less without the larger discount. Discount rates also differ widely between salespeople, from 4.8% (Janet Leverling) to 11.8% (Robert King). Robert King also has the highest rate when every line counts equally (7.4%).

Repeat purchases: 83% of discounted orders were followed by another order from the same customer within 90 days, versus 64% for full-price orders. To avoid right-censoring, only orders placed on or before 1998-02-05 are counted, so each one has a full 90-day follow-up period in the data (294 discounted and 331 full-price orders). This is an association: frequent buyers may simply be the customers who receive discounts. 8 lines with rare discount levels (1–4% and 6%) are left out of the chart.

Business implication

The data does not show that discounts above 10% come with extra volume compared with smaller discounts. Northwind could test a 10% standard cap with manager approval above it, and agree on common discount guidelines for the sales team. Because this is observational data, the cap should first be piloted for one quarter with some sales reps or customers, checking whether order volumes and repeat purchases hold.

Data, method and limitations

  • Data: the Northwind Database workbook provided for the course (Orders, Order Details, Customers, Products, Employees). Revenue = unit price × quantity × (1 − discount), using the price actually charged on each order.
  • Like-for-like comparison: the data runs from 1996-07-04 to 1998-05-06. Year-over-year comparisons use Jan–Apr 1997 vs Jan–Apr 1998, so seasonality and partial months do not distort them.
  • No cost data: the dataset has no purchase costs, so profit and margin cannot be measured. All findings are about revenue.
  • Small groups: many countries have only 1–5 customers, so country-level changes can come from a single order.
  • Observational data: discount effects are associations, not proven causes. The recommended cap should be tested before it is rolled out.
